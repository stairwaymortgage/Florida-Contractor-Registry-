Building Permits in Alachua County, FL: How They Work

Learn how building permits work in Alachua County, FL when you hire a contractor. What's required, who pulls the permit, and how to protect yourself.

Building permits in Florida work as an official oversight system: a licensed contractor applies for a permit through the local building authority before work begins, inspectors review the work at key stages, and the jurisdiction closes the permit once the project passes final inspection. In Alachua County, this process runs through the county's Building Division and applies to most structural, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work on residential and commercial properties.

What is a building permit, and why does it matter to you as a homeowner?

A building permit is an official authorization from your local government — in this case Alachua County or a city within it such as Gainesville — that allows specific construction or renovation work to proceed. It matters because it creates an inspection record that protects your investment. When work is completed under an open permit and passes inspections, you have documented evidence that the work met the Florida Building Code at the time it was performed. Without that record, unpermitted work can complicate a home sale, affect your homeowner's insurance, or require costly remediation later.

Who pulls the permit — the contractor or the homeowner?

Your licensed contractor is responsible for pulling the permit in almost every residential project. Florida law requires contractors to be licensed specifically so they can take legal responsibility for permitted work. When a contractor pulls a permit, their license number is attached to the project, which means they are accountable to the state if the work fails inspection or violates code.

A homeowner can pull an owner-builder permit for work on their own primary residence, but this comes with trade-offs. Acting as your own general contractor means you assume the legal and financial risk that a licensed contractor would otherwise carry. This path also affects certain consumer protections under Florida law. Before choosing owner-builder status, speak with the Alachua County Building Division and, if needed, a construction attorney.

How does the permit process work in Alachua County?

The process in Alachua County follows the general Florida Building Code framework, with local administrative steps layered on top. Here is a general sequence for a typical residential project:

  1. Contractor submits application. Your contractor files a permit application with the Alachua County Building Division, along with any required plans or drawings.
  2. Plans review. Building officials review the submitted plans for compliance with the Florida Building Code and any local amendments.
  3. Permit issued. Once approved, the permit is issued and work may begin. The permit number should be posted or available at the job site.
  4. Inspections during construction. Inspectors visit at designated stages — for example, after framing is complete but before drywall is installed — to verify code compliance.
  5. Final inspection. After all work is finished, an inspector conducts a final review. Passing this inspection closes the permit.
  6. Certificate of occupancy (if applicable). For new construction or significant additions, a certificate of occupancy is issued confirming the space is safe to occupy.

Timelines vary based on project type, application volume, and completeness of submitted documents. Contact the Alachua County Building Division directly for current processing times.

What types of projects require a permit in Alachua County?

Most work that affects the structure, systems, or safety of a building requires a permit. Common examples include:

  • Additions, room conversions, or new structures
  • Roof replacements (in most cases)
  • Electrical panel upgrades or new circuits
  • Plumbing changes, including water heater replacements in many jurisdictions
  • HVAC system replacements or new installations
  • Window or door replacements that change the opening size
  • Fences and pools, depending on type and location

Cosmetic work — painting, flooring, cabinet replacement — typically does not require a permit. When in doubt, call the Alachua County Building Division before work starts. Getting a definitive answer costs nothing and can save significant trouble later.

How do you verify that your contractor is actually licensed before the permit is pulled?

Verifying your contractor's license is a step you should complete before signing a contract, not after. Florida contractor licenses are public records under Chapter 119, Florida Statutes, maintained by the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR). The state's own DBPR website is the authoritative source for official verification.

If a contractor cannot produce a license number or discourages you from looking them up, treat that as a serious warning sign. Licensed contractors in Florida are required to include their license number on contracts and advertising.

What should you do if something goes wrong after the permit is closed?

If you believe a contractor performed work that did not meet code — or that an inspection was improperly passed — you have options. Florida's DBPR accepts complaints against licensed contractors, and Alachua County's Building Division can address inspection-related concerns. You can also review the complaint process for guidance on how to document and report issues with a contractor's licensed work.

Keeping your own copies of the permit number, inspection records, and contractor communications from the start of the project makes any future dispute easier to navigate. Official records of permitted work are generally public records in Florida and can be requested from the issuing jurisdiction.

Common questions

Who is legally responsible for pulling the building permit — the homeowner or the contractor?
In Florida, the licensed contractor is generally responsible for pulling the building permit for work they perform. A homeowner may pull an owner-builder permit for their own primary residence under specific conditions defined by Florida law, but doing so removes certain consumer protections. Consult the Alachua County Building Division or a construction attorney if you are unsure which path applies to your project.
What happens if work is done without a permit in Florida?
Unpermitted work can create serious problems when you sell your home, refinance, or file an insurance claim, because the work has no official inspection record. A local building authority can require you to open walls or reverse completed work to allow inspection after the fact. In Alachua County, you may also face stop-work orders or fines until the situation is resolved.
Can I check whether my contractor has an active Florida license before work starts?
Yes. Florida contractor licenses are public records maintained by the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR). You can search official DBPR records through the state's own website, or use Florida Contractor Registry — a private directory built from those same public records — to review license status before signing any contract.
Does every home improvement project in Florida require a permit?
Not every project requires a permit, but most structural, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC work does. Minor cosmetic work such as painting or replacing cabinet hardware typically does not. The Alachua County Building Division can tell you whether your specific project triggers the permit requirement under the Florida Building Code.
What is a certificate of occupancy and when do I need one?
A certificate of occupancy (CO) is an official document issued by the local building authority confirming that a structure or addition meets code and is safe to occupy. It is typically required after new construction or major renovations before you or a tenant can legally move into the space. In Alachua County, the CO is issued only after all required inspections have passed.
How long does a Florida building permit stay valid?
Under the Florida Building Code, a permit generally expires if work does not begin within a set period after issuance, or if the work is abandoned for an extended time without an approved inspection. The exact timeframes are set by the Florida Building Code and can be confirmed with your local Alachua County building office. Extensions may be available under certain circumstances.
What should I do if my contractor says I don't need a permit?
Contact the Alachua County Building Division directly and describe the scope of work — do not rely solely on your contractor's assertion. If a permit is required and one was not pulled, both you and your contractor could face legal and financial consequences. This situation can also be grounds for a complaint against a contractor's license.
